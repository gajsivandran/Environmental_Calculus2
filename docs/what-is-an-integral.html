Environmental Calculus 2 - Active Learning with Case Studies

Chapter 3 What Is an Integral?

A stream gauge logs flow every 15 minutes during a storm. Each reading tells you how fast water is moving past, in cubic meters per second. The people downstream don’t care much about any single reading. They want to know how much water went by. To get that, you add up all the changing flow rates across the storm. That’s an integral.

This chapter builds the integral one piece at a time. We start with rates you can picture, turn them into areas under a curve, then write the whole process as a Riemann sum.

3.1 Chapter Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Explain how an integral turns a rate into a total, using examples like streamflow, photosynthesis and pollutant load
  • Interpret the area under a rate curve as total change, including gains, losses and net change
  • Construct and evaluate left, right and midpoint Riemann sums from a graph, a formula or a table of data
  • Apply the trapezoidal rule, and explain why it often beats rectangles
  • Choose an approximation method, and say whether it will overestimate or underestimate

3.2 Accumulation in Environmental Systems

Most of what you measure in the field is a rate: how fast something is happening. An integral turns a rate into a total.

Some examples:

  • Streamflow → total river discharge over a day
  • Photosynthesis rate → total daily carbon uptake
  • Pollutant concentration × flow → total pollutant load entering a lake
  • Evapotranspiration → total water loss during a heatwave
  • Recruitment rate → total new individuals added to a population

If you know the rate something changes, the integral gives you the total change over an interval.

Walking on a Treadmill

Start with something you can feel. You’re on a treadmill at a steady 5 miles per hour. That speed is a rate: it tells you how fast distance is being added.

Walk at 5 mph for 1 hour and your total distance is:

\[ \text{distance} = \text{rate} \times \text{time} = 5 \times 1 = 5 \text{ miles}. \]

Now say the speed changes: 4 mph for the warm-up, 5 mph in the middle, 6 mph at the end. You can still get the total distance. Each minute at 4 mph adds a bit of distance, each minute at 5 mph adds a bit more, and each minute at 6 mph adds more again. Add up all the minutes and you have the total distance walked.

That’s what an integral does. It adds up a changing rate over time.

Concept Check: What an Integral Represents

In your own words, what does an integral represent in environmental science?

Click to reveal answer

An integral gives the total accumulation of a quantity over time or space when you know its rate of change. It adds up many small contributions to get a total: total volume of water, total carbon fixed, total mass exported.

Concept Check: Why Rates Are Easier Than Totals

Why are rates often easier to measure directly than total accumulated quantities?

Click to reveal answer

A rate can be measured in a moment or over a short interval. A stream gauge logs discharge every few minutes, and a flux tower logs CO₂ uptake many times a second. A total means tracking accumulation over the whole period, and that’s the job the integral does for you.

3.3 From Rate Curves to Areas Under Curves

When you graph a rate \(f(t)\), the height of the curve is how fast the system is changing at each moment.

The total accumulation from time \(a\) to \(b\) is the area under the rate curve:

  • Height (rate) × width (time) = accumulated amount
  • Units multiply:
    • \(\text{m}^3/\text{s} \times \text{s} = \text{m}^3\)
    • \(\mu\text{mol}/(\text{m}^2\cdot\text{s}) \times \text{s} = \mu\text{mol}/\text{m}^2\)

Environmental examples:

  • Total river discharge from a storm hydrograph
  • Total CO₂ absorbed during a sunny day
  • Total nitrogen mineralized over a growing season

Filling a Water Bottle

You’re filling a water bottle from a tap, and the flow rate changes:

  • You start gently: 50 mL/s for 10 seconds.
  • You open the tap further: 90 mL/s for 15 seconds.
  • You ease it back near the top: 30 mL/s for 10 seconds.

To find how much water ends up in the bottle, you can’t multiply one rate by one time. You add up each stretch: rate × time for the slow start, the fast middle and the slow finish. On the graph, each stretch is a rectangle under the rate curve. Add the rectangles and you have the total volume in the bottle.

That’s what an integral measures: the total you get by adding up small contributions from a changing rate.

Concept Check: Area Under a Rate Curve

What does the area under a rate curve represent?

Click to reveal answer

The area under a rate curve represents the total accumulated change of the quantity over the interval shown. If the rate is in m³/s and the horizontal axis is in seconds, the area is in m³: a volume.

Concept Check: Why the Units Work Out

Why do the units of an integral make sense physically?

Click to reveal answer

The integral multiplies a rate by a width (time or distance), so the units simplify to a total. For example, m³/hr × hr = m³, and mg C/m²/day × day = mg C/m². The “per time” or “per distance” in the rate cancels with the variable you’re integrating over.

Concept Check: Why a Single Rate Times Time Falls Short

Why is multiplying a single rate by total time often a poor approximation in environmental systems?

Click to reveal answer

Because environmental rates usually change over time. Streamflow rises and falls during a storm, photosynthesis follows the light, and soil respiration follows temperature. One reading doesn’t represent the whole interval, so multiplying it by the full duration throws away all that variation.

3.4 Riemann Sums: Adding Up Small Pieces

Real rates are not constant, so one rate times one time won’t do. Instead, break the interval into small pieces and add up what each piece contributes:

  1. Divide the interval into smaller subintervals of equal width.
  2. Approximate the rate as constant over each subinterval.
  3. Compute each slice’s contribution using
    \[ \text{(height)} \times \text{(width)}. \]
  4. Add all slices together to estimate the total accumulation.

Each slice is the amount accumulated over a short stretch of time. Add them all and you have an estimate of the total.

3.4.1 Choosing a Riemann Sum Method

Left, right and midpoint sums differ in one thing: where in each slice you read the rate. That choice is an assumption about how the system behaves inside each slice.

  • Left Riemann sum. Uses the rate at the left endpoint of each slice. It assumes the rate holds at its starting value for the whole slice. It’s the natural choice when your logger records at the start of each interval.
    • Rate increasing: underestimates.
    • Rate decreasing: overestimates.
  • Right Riemann sum. Uses the rate at the right endpoint. It assumes the rate has already reached its end value across the whole slice. It’s the natural choice when data are recorded at the end of each interval.
    • Rate increasing: overestimates.
    • Rate decreasing: underestimates.
  • Midpoint Riemann sum. Uses the rate at the middle of each slice. For a smooth rate, the middle value is usually close to the slice’s average, so the errors on either side of it partly cancel. With the same number of slices, it’s usually much closer than left or right.

Rule of thumb: use the midpoint for smooth processes when you can. Use left or right when that’s how your data were recorded, or when you want an estimate you know is high or low. If accuracy matters, use more slices.

Concept Check: What a Single Rectangle Represents

What does each rectangle in a Riemann sum represent?

Click to reveal answer

Each rectangle is a small contribution to the total. Its width is a short slice of time (or distance), and its height is the rate during that slice. Multiply them and you get roughly how much accumulated in that slice.

Concept Check: Why More Rectangles Helps

Why do Riemann sums become more accurate as the number of rectangles increases?

Click to reveal answer

Narrower slices leave less room for the rate to change inside each rectangle. Assuming the rate is constant is more reasonable over a short slice, so the estimate closes in on the true total.

Concept Check: The Assumption Inside Each Rectangle

What assumption is made when using rectangular approximations?

Click to reveal answer

That the rate stays roughly constant within each subinterval. Left endpoint, right endpoint or midpoint, you’re using one value to stand in for the rate across the whole slice.

3.5 Approximation and Error in Riemann Sums

A Riemann sum is an approximation. Swapping a changing rate for flat rectangles introduces error, because the rectangles don’t match the shape of the curve. Some stick up above it, others fall short. The gap between the rectangles’ total area and the true area under the curve is the approximation error.

3.5.1 Why Error Occurs

Inside each slice, we treat the rate as constant, but the real rate is rising, falling or curving. The more it changes within a slice, the bigger the error. That’s why narrower slices, and more of them, give a better estimate. Shrink the slice width toward zero and the Riemann sum approaches the definite integral: the exact total.

3.5.2 Overestimates and Underestimates

Whether a Riemann sum overestimates or underestimates depends on two things: the shape of the rate curve, and where you read the rate in each slice.

3.5.2.1 Increasing Rate Functions

When the rate is increasing:

  • Left sums underestimate. Each rectangle uses the smallest rate in its slice.
  • Right sums overestimate. Each rectangle uses the largest rate in its slice.
  • Midpoint sums land in between, usually close to the true value.

3.5.2.2 Decreasing Rate Functions

When the rate is decreasing, everything flips:

  • Left sums overestimate.
  • Right sums underestimate.
  • Midpoint sums again usually land closest.

Concept Check: Why Left Sums Underestimate Increasing Rates

Why does a left Riemann sum tend to underestimate accumulation for an increasing function?

Click to reveal answer

It uses the rate at the start of each interval, which is lower than the rate everywhere else in that interval. Every rectangle is too short, and the shortfalls add up across all the subintervals.

Concept Check: Why Right Sums Overestimate Increasing Rates

Why does a right Riemann sum tend to overestimate accumulation for an increasing function?

Click to reveal answer

It uses the rate at the end of each interval, which is higher than the rate everywhere else in that interval. Every rectangle is too tall, and the excess adds up across all the subintervals.

Concept Check: Why the Midpoint Tends to Win

Why is the midpoint Riemann sum often more accurate than left or right sums?

Click to reveal answer

The midpoint value is usually close to the average rate over the subinterval. For a smooth function, the part of the rectangle that overshoots the curve on one side mostly cancels the part that falls short on the other.

3.5.3 Interpreting Error in Environmental Contexts

In practice, the error has consequences. Underestimate a pollutant load and cleanup gets delayed. Overestimate the water available and you end up with a management plan that can’t be met.

Often the goal isn’t the exact number. It’s a number you can defend. So the question to ask is:

Which is worse here: guessing too high, or too low?

A conservative estimate errs on the safe side. Which side is safe depends on the problem:

  • Too low is safer for water supply (don’t promise water you can’t deliver), carbon offsets (don’t overstate the benefit) and population recovery (don’t declare success early).
  • Too high is safer for pollutant loads (size the treatment big enough), flood volumes (design the culvert for more) and demand forecasts (don’t run short).

If the rate is only rising or only falling over the interval, left and right sums give you a guaranteed low estimate and a guaranteed high one. You can pick the one that’s safe on purpose. The midpoint aims for accuracy instead. Either way, picking a method is a modeling decision.

Concept Check: What Makes an Estimate Conservative

What makes an estimate “conservative” in environmental modeling?

Click to reveal answer

A conservative estimate errs on the side that does less harm if it turns out to be wrong. For a pollutant load, that might mean deliberately overestimating so treatment is big enough. For a sustainable harvest, it might mean underestimating so you don’t take more than the system can support. Which way is “conservative” depends on which mistake would hurt more.

Concept Check: Choosing a Method Is a Modeling Decision

Why is choosing a Riemann sum method considered a modeling decision rather than a purely mathematical one?

Click to reveal answer

Because the choice rests on assumptions about timing, uncertainty and acceptable risk, and those depend on what happens if you’re wrong. Someone calculating a pollutant load for a permit may deliberately pick the method that overestimates. A fisheries manager setting a catch limit may deliberately pick the one that underestimates. Same math, different judgment.

3.6 Riemann Sums as a Calculus Expression

So far we’ve described Riemann sums with words, pictures and rectangles. That’s enough to see what’s going on. To work with any function on any interval, we need a compact way to write “add up many small pieces.”

That’s summation notation.

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \]

It lets you write the whole process in one expression: break the interval into pieces, estimate each piece’s contribution, and add them up.

3.6.1 Rectangle Contributions in Notation

To build a Riemann sum, we first divide the interval \([a,b]\) into \(n\) subintervals of equal width \(\Delta x\).

In each subinterval, we choose a sample point \(x_i^*\).
The rectangle over that subinterval has:

  • height: \(f(x_i^*)\)
  • width: \(\Delta x\)

Each rectangle therefore contributes approximately \[ f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x \] to the total accumulation.

3.6.1.1 Width of the Rectangle

The interval from \(a\) to \(b\) has length \(b - a\). Split it into \(n\) equal pieces and each piece has width \[ \Delta x = \frac{b - a}{n}. \]

\(\Delta x\) is the width of each rectangle. As \(n\) goes up, \(\Delta x\) gets smaller and the estimate gets better.

3.6.1.2 Determining the Height of Each Rectangle

The height of each rectangle is the rate at a chosen sample point in its subinterval. For the \(i\)-th subinterval, pick a point \(x_i^*\). The rectangle’s height is \[ f(x_i^*). \]

Where you put \(x_i^*\) is what makes it a left, right or midpoint sum: at the left end, the right end or the center of the subinterval.

3.6.1.3 Combining Width and Height

Height \(f(x_i^*)\) is the rate. Width \(\Delta x\) is the length of the slice. Multiply them: \[ \text{area of one rectangle} \;\approx\; f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x. \]

That’s just \[ \text{amount} = \text{rate} \times \text{time}, \] done once per slice instead of once for the whole interval. Do it for every slice, add them up, and you have your estimate.

3.6.2 The Riemann Sum - Adding the Rectangles

Put the pieces together and a Riemann sum is written as \[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x. \]

Reading it piece by piece:

  • \(i = 1\) to \(n\): add over all the subintervals
  • \(f(x_i^*)\): the height of the \(i\)-th rectangle
  • \(\Delta x\): the width, the same for every rectangle

Each term is the accumulation over one slice. The whole sum estimates the total accumulation of \(f(x)\) over \([a,b]\). The more slices you use, the better the estimate.

3.6.3 Left, Right, and Midpoint Riemann Sums in Notation

The choice of sample point \(x_i^*\) determines the type of Riemann sum:

  • Left Riemann sum:
    \[ x_i^* = a + (i - 1)\Delta x \]

  • Right Riemann sum:
    \[ x_i^* = a + i\Delta x \]

  • Midpoint Riemann sum:
    \[ x_i^* = a + \left(i - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\Delta x \]

These are the same left, right and midpoint rectangles from the pictures earlier, just written down.

3.6.4 Example 1: Approximating Accumulation with a Left Riemann Sum

The next few examples all use the same scenario, so you can compare methods side by side.

Scenario: A downspout filling a rain barrel as a storm builds

A flow sensor on a roof downspout logs the rate \(Q(t)\) at which water runs into a rain barrel: \[ Q(t) = t^2 \quad \text{liters per minute}, \] where \(t\) is minutes since the sensor started recording. The rain is getting heavier, so the flow keeps rising. How much water went into the barrel between \(t = 1\) and \(t = 7\) minutes?

The total volume is the area under the \(Q(t)\) curve on \([1, 7]\). We’ll estimate it with a left-hand Riemann sum using \(n = 3\) subintervals.

3.6.4.1 Step 1: Compute the Width \(\Delta t\)

The width of each subinterval is \[ \Delta t = \frac{b-a}{n} = \frac{7 - 1}{3} = 2 \text{ minutes}. \]

3.6.4.2 Step 2: Identify the Left Endpoints

For a left Riemann sum, the sample points are \[ t_i^* = a + (i - 1)\Delta t. \]

With \(a = 1\) and \(\Delta t = 2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} t_1^* &= 1 + (1 - 1)(2) = 1, \\ t_2^* &= 1 + (2 - 1)(2) = 3, \\ t_3^* &= 1 + (3 - 1)(2) = 5. \end{aligned} \]

These are the left endpoints of the subintervals \[ [1,3],\quad [3,5],\quad [5,7] \text{ minutes}. \]

3.6.4.3 Step 3: Evaluate the Flow Rate at Each Left Endpoint

\[ Q(1) = 1^2 = 1,\quad Q(3) = 3^2 = 9,\quad Q(5) = 5^2 = 25 \quad (\text{all in L/min}). \]

3.6.4.4 Step 4: Factoring Out \(\Delta t\)

Every term in the sum has the same \(\Delta t\), so you can factor it out.

Starting from \[ \sum_{i=1}^{3} Q(t_i^*)\,\Delta t, \] we rewrite this as \[ \Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{3} Q(t_i^*). \]

With \(\Delta t = 2\), this becomes \[ 2 \sum_{i=1}^{3} Q(t_i^*) = 2(1 + 9 + 25). \]

Add up the heights (flow rates in L/min), then multiply by the width (minutes) to turn them into a volume (liters):

\[ 2 \times 35 = 70 \text{ liters}. \]

3.6.4.5 Interpretation

The left-hand sum estimates that 70 liters went into the barrel between minute 1 and minute 7.

Because \(Q(t)\) is increasing on this interval (the rain is getting heavier), each left-endpoint height is the lowest flow in its slice. So this estimate underestimates the true volume.

3.6.5 Example 2: Right-Hand Riemann Sum

Same scenario: \(Q(t) = t^2\) L/min on \([1, 7]\) minutes, with \(n = 3\). This time we use the right endpoints.

3.6.5.1 Step 1: Compute the Width \(\Delta t\)

\[ \Delta t = \frac{7 - 1}{3} = 2 \text{ minutes}. \]

3.6.5.2 Step 2: Identify the Right Endpoints

For a right Riemann sum, the sample points are \[ t_i^* = a + i\,\Delta t. \]

With \(a=1\) and \(\Delta t=2\): \[ \begin{aligned} t_1^* &= 1 + 1(2) = 3,\\ t_2^* &= 1 + 2(2) = 5,\\ t_3^* &= 1 + 3(2) = 7. \end{aligned} \]

3.6.5.3 Step 3: Evaluate the Flow Rate

\[ Q(3)=9,\quad Q(5)=25,\quad Q(7)=49 \quad (\text{L/min}). \]

3.6.5.4 Step 4: Form the Riemann Sum

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{3} Q(t_i^*)\,\Delta t = (9 + 25 + 49)(2) = 83 \times 2 = 166 \text{ liters}. \]

3.6.5.5 Interpretation

The right-hand sum estimates 166 liters.

\(Q(t)\) is increasing, so each right-endpoint height is the highest flow in its slice. This estimate overestimates the true volume.

3.6.6 Example 3: Midpoint Riemann Sum

Now we use the midpoint of each subinterval as the sample point.

3.6.6.1 Step 1: Width \(\Delta t\)

\[ \Delta t = 2 \text{ minutes}. \]

3.6.6.2 Step 2: Identify the Midpoints

For a midpoint Riemann sum, the sample points are \[ t_i^* = a + \left(i - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\Delta t. \]

With \(a=1\) and \(\Delta t=2\): \[ \begin{aligned} t_1^* &= 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}(2) = 2,\\ t_2^* &= 1 + \tfrac{3}{2}(2) = 4,\\ t_3^* &= 1 + \tfrac{5}{2}(2) = 6. \end{aligned} \]

3.6.6.3 Step 3: Evaluate the Flow Rate

\[ Q(2)=4,\quad Q(4)=16,\quad Q(6)=36 \quad (\text{L/min}). \]

3.6.6.4 Step 4: Form the Riemann Sum

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{3} Q(t_i^*)\,\Delta t = (4 + 16 + 36)(2) = 56 \times 2 = 112 \text{ liters}. \]

3.6.6.5 Interpretation

The midpoint sum estimates 112 liters. It lands between the left and right sums, and much closer to the truth than either.

3.6.7 Comparison

Same curve, same interval, same \(n = 3\). The only difference between the panels is where each rectangle’s height is read.

Because \(Q(t)=t^2\) is increasing on \([1,7]\) (the rain is getting heavier), you can predict which way each method will miss:

  • Left Riemann sum (left panel):
    Heights come from the left endpoint. Flow is rising, so the rectangles sit below the curve: an underestimate.

  • Right Riemann sum (right panel):
    Heights come from the right endpoint, so the rectangles stick up above the curve: an overestimate.

  • Midpoint Riemann sum (middle panel):
    Heights come from the middle of each slice. Each rectangle is a little high on one side and a little low on the other, and the two mostly cancel: a much closer estimate.

Click to reveal answer

The rectangles don’t match the shape of the curve. Whenever the rate changes within a subinterval, a flat rectangle has to overshoot or fall short, and that mismatch is the error.

3.7 Refining the Approximation

How good a Riemann sum is depends on how finely you slice. Wide slices miss changes in the rate. Narrow slices follow the curve more closely, so the gaps between the rectangle tops and the curve shrink, and so does the error.

Keep going. As the slice width goes to zero and the number of slices grows without limit, the Riemann sum settles on a single value. That value is the definite integral: the exact total. You’ll work with it properly in Chapter 4.

3.7.1 Increasing \(n\)

So far we’ve changed the method and kept \(n\) fixed. Now flip it: keep the method, and increase the number of rectangles.

Back to the rain barrel from Examples 1 to 3: \[ Q(t) = t^2 \quad \text{liters per minute}, \] on \([1, 7]\) minutes. Here are left-hand Riemann sums with more and more subintervals: \[ n = 3,\quad n = 8,\quad n = 20. \]

All three use left endpoints, so any change in the estimate comes from the width \(\Delta t\) alone.

Same function, same interval, same method. Only the number of subintervals changes.

\(Q(t) = t^2\) is increasing, so every one of these left sums is an underestimate. What changes is how big the miss is.

3.7.1.1 Case 1: \(n = 3\)

The width of each subinterval is \[ \Delta t = \frac{7 - 1}{3} = 2 \text{ minutes}. \]

The left endpoints are \[ t_1^* = 1,\quad t_2^* = 3,\quad t_3^* = 5. \]

The left-hand Riemann sum is \[ \sum_{i=1}^{3} Q(t_i^*)\,\Delta t = 2\,[1^2 + 3^2 + 5^2] = 2(1 + 9 + 25) = 70 \text{ L}. \]

Low, because the rectangles are wide and the flow climbs fast across each one.

3.7.1.2 Case 2: \(n = 8\)

The width of each subinterval is \[ \Delta t = \frac{7 - 1}{8} = \frac{6}{8} = 0.75 \text{ minutes}. \]

The left endpoints are \[ t_i^* = 1 + (i-1)(0.75), \quad i = 1,\dots,8. \]

The left-hand Riemann sum is \[ \sum_{i=1}^{8} Q(t_i^*)\,\Delta t = 0.75 \sum_{i=1}^{8} \bigl(1 + (i-1)(0.75)\bigr)^2 \approx 96.56 \text{ L}. \]

Still low, but the narrower rectangles track the rising flow better and the error shrinks a lot.

3.7.1.3 Case 3: \(n = 20\)

The width of each subinterval is \[ \Delta t = \frac{7 - 1}{20} = 0.3 \text{ minutes}. \]

The left endpoints are \[ t_i^* = 1 + (i-1)(0.3), \quad i = 1,\dots,20. \]

The left-hand Riemann sum is \[ \sum_{i=1}^{20} Q(t_i^*)\,\Delta t = 0.3 \sum_{i=1}^{20} \bigl(1 + (i-1)(0.3)\bigr)^2 \approx 106.89 \text{ L}. \]

With 20 narrow rectangles, treating the flow as constant in each slice is a reasonable assumption, and the estimate is close to the true volume.

3.7.1.4 Comparison with the True Volume

The exact volume is the definite integral. You’ll learn how to get this number in Chapter 4. For now, take it as given: \[ \int_1^7 t^2\,dt = \left[\frac{t^3}{3}\right]_1^7 = \frac{343 - 1}{3} = 114 \text{ L}. \]

Comparing all four values:

  • Left Riemann sum with \(n=3\): \(70 \text{ L}\)
  • Left Riemann sum with \(n=8\): \(\approx 96.56 \text{ L}\)
  • Left Riemann sum with \(n=20\): \(\approx 106.89 \text{ L}\)
  • Exact volume: \(114 \text{ L}\)

The left sums climb toward the true value from below as \(n\) increases. Take \(n \to \infty\), so \(\Delta t \to 0\), and the Riemann sum becomes the definite integral: the exact volume.

3.8 Beyond Rectangles: More Accurate Approximation Methods

Rectangles assume the rate is flat across each slice. When the rate changes smoothly, you can often do better without adding more slices: use a shape that follows the curve. This section covers the trapezoidal rule. You’ll meet Simpson’s rule, which fits curves instead of straight lines, in Chapter 6.

3.9 The Trapezoidal Rule

The trapezoidal rule swaps rectangles for trapezoids. Instead of assuming the rate is constant across each slice, it assumes the rate changes in a straight line from one end of the slice to the other.

3.9.1 Idea

On each subinterval \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\), draw a straight line from \((x_{i-1}, f(x_{i-1}))\) to \((x_i, f(x_i))\). The area under that line is a trapezoid.

In effect, it averages the left and right values, which cancels out much of the built-in bias of left and right sums.

Same function, interval and partition in both panels. The left-sum rectangles sit flat at the left endpoint, so on a rising curve they come up short on every slice. The trapezoids join both endpoints with a straight line, so they follow the rise within each slice and land much closer.

So there are two ways to improve an estimate: use more slices, or use a better shape for each slice.

3.9.2 Area of a Single Trapezoid

The area of a trapezoid with:

  • base width \(\Delta x\),
  • left height \(f(x_{i-1})\),
  • right height \(f(x_i)\),

is given by \[ \text{Area of trapezoid} = \frac{1}{2} \bigl[f(x_{i-1}) + f(x_i)\bigr]\Delta x. \]

In words: the average of the two heights, times the width.

3.9.3 Adding the Trapezoids Together

To cover \([a,b]\), divide it into \(n\) equal subintervals of width \[ \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}. \]

Each subinterval contributes one trapezoid. Adding the areas of all trapezoids gives: \[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{2} \bigl[f(x_{i-1}) + f(x_i)\bigr]\Delta x. \]

Factoring out the common constants, \[ \frac{\Delta x}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl[f(x_{i-1}) + f(x_i)\bigr]. \]

Write out the terms and you get the trapezoidal pattern: \[ \frac{\Delta x}{2} \left[ f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + 2f(x_2) + \cdots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n) \right]. \]

3.9.4 Interpretation

  • The first and last values appear once, because each belongs to only one trapezoid.
  • Every value in between appears twice: it’s the right side of one trapezoid and the left side of the next.
  • For smooth functions, this is usually much more accurate than a left or right sum.

3.9.5 Example: Trapezoidal Rule

Same rain barrel: \(Q(t) = t^2\) L/min on \([1, 7]\) minutes, \(n = 3\). This time with trapezoids.

3.9.5.1 Step 1: Compute the Width \(\Delta t\)

\[ \Delta t = \frac{7 - 1}{3} = 2 \text{ minutes}. \]

3.9.5.2 Step 2: Identify the Partition Points

The trapezoidal rule uses both endpoints of each subinterval, so you need all the partition points: \[ \begin{aligned} t_0 &= 1,\\ t_1 &= 3,\\ t_2 &= 5,\\ t_3 &= 7. \end{aligned} \]

3.9.5.3 Step 3: Evaluate the Flow Rate at Each Partition Point

\[ \begin{aligned} Q(1) &= 1,\\ Q(3) &= 9,\\ Q(5) &= 25,\\ Q(7) &= 49 \quad (\text{L/min}). \end{aligned} \]

3.9.5.4 Step 4: Apply the Trapezoidal Rule Formula

The formula is: \[ \text{Volume} \approx \frac{\Delta t}{2} \left[ Q(t_0) + 2\,Q(t_1) + 2\,Q(t_2) + Q(t_3) \right]. \]

Substitute the values: \[ \frac{2}{2} \left[ 1 + 2(9) + 2(25) + 49 \right]. \]

Simplify: \[ 1 \cdot \left(1 + 18 + 50 + 49\right) = 118 \text{ liters}. \]

3.9.5.5 Final Estimate

The trapezoidal rule estimates the volume as \[ \boxed{118 \text{ L}}. \]

3.9.5.6 Interpretation

Because the trapezoids follow the rise in flow within each slice, this estimate is much closer than the left or right sum.

Here are all the estimates for the volume that went into the barrel between \(t = 1\) and \(t = 7\) minutes. The true volume is: \[ \int_1^7 t^2\,dt = \frac{1}{3}\left(7^3 - 1^3\right) = \frac{342}{3} = 114 \text{ liters}. \]

3.10 Accuracy Trade-Offs: Shape vs. Number of Subintervals

There are two ways to make an estimate more accurate:

  1. Use a better shape for each subinterval
  2. Use more subintervals

Both work. They cost different things.

3.10.1 Improving Accuracy by Changing the Shape

Left and right sums treat the rate as constant in each slice. The trapezoidal rule lets it change in a straight line. The midpoint rule reads the rate where it’s usually closest to the slice’s average.

With \(n = 3\) in our example, left and right were off by 44 and 52 liters. Midpoint and trapezoid were off by 2 and 4. Same number of slices, same amount of data, much better answer.

3.10.2 Improving Accuracy by Increasing \(n\)

The other option is to keep the method and add slices. Over a short enough slice, even a rectangle does fine, and as \(n \to \infty\) every method lands on the true value.

The catch is cost. More slices means more arithmetic, which gets old fast by hand. With real data, it means measuring more often, and that isn’t always possible.

3.10.3 Comparing the Two Strategies

Strategy Advantage Limitation
Better shape (midpoint, trapezoid) High accuracy with small \(n\) More complex formulas
Larger \(n\) Conceptually simple, universally applicable More computation and data

In practice you usually do both: a moderate \(n\) with a better method.

3.10.4 A Modeling Perspective

Which to lean on depends on your data. If measurements are sparse, a better shape gets more out of what you have. If you have lots of data, or a computer is doing the arithmetic, more slices is easy. And if the risk of being wrong matters more than accuracy, you may still pick a left or right sum on purpose.

Concept Check: Shape vs. Subdivision

Why can changing the shape of the approximation be more effective than just increasing \(n\)?

Click to reveal answer

A better shape captures more of what the function does in each subinterval. A trapezoid can match a smooth curve with far fewer pieces than rectangles, so you get the same accuracy with less work and less data.

3.11 Negative Area and Signed Accumulation

So far every rate has been positive: the quantity only goes up. Real systems also lose things: outflow, evaporation, decay, death. Calculus handles losses with signed area.

3.11.1 What Is a Negative Area?

When a rate curve drops below the horizontal axis, the rate is negative. The area between the curve and the axis then counts as negative. It isn’t a negative area in the geometric sense. It’s a loss.

So accumulation is signed: area above the axis adds to the total, and area below the axis subtracts from it.

3.11.2 Why This Makes Sense Conceptually

A positive rate means the quantity is going up. A negative rate means it’s going down. The integral, or a Riemann sum, adds up all the changes, losses included. The result is the net change.

Some examples:

  • Evaporation exceeding precipitation (net water loss)
  • Respiration exceeding photosynthesis (net carbon loss)
  • Pollutant removal or decay (negative loading)
  • Population decline (negative growth rate)

3.11.3 A Simple Example

Here’s a simplified model of a forest’s net carbon flux in the hours after dawn, with positive meaning carbon going into the forest. Let \[ F(t) = t - 2 \quad \text{g C / m}^2\text{/ hr}, \] on the interval \(t \in [0, 4]\) hours after dawn.

  • For \(t < 2\) (early morning), \(F(t)\) is negative. Respiration beats photosynthesis, so the forest is a carbon source.
  • At \(t = 2\), the two balance.
  • For \(t > 2\), \(F(t)\) is positive. Photosynthesis wins, so the forest is a carbon sink.

The net total depends on both regions.

3.11.4 Interpretation of the Result

  • From \(t = 0\) to \(t = 2\), \(F(t)\) is negative. That triangle has area \(\tfrac{1}{2}(2)(2) = 2\), so the forest lost 2 g C/m².
  • From \(t = 2\) to \(t = 4\), \(F(t)\) is positive. Same-sized triangle, so the forest gained 2 g C/m².
  • The two cancel exactly: net change is zero.

Net zero doesn’t mean nothing happened. The forest released carbon for two hours and took up the same amount over the next two.

3.11.5 Why Signed Accumulation Matters

Most real systems gain and lose at the same time, or take turns. A lake gains water in storms and loses it to evaporation. A forest fixes carbon by day and respires it at night. A population grows in good seasons and shrinks in bad ones. Because integrals are signed, they give you the net change in all of these.
